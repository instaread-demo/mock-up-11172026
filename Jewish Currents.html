Stephen Miller’s Zombie Politics

Miller’s ideology may be dead, but thanks to his big promotion, it carries on like a reanimated corpse.

David Klion
November 13, 2026

In an interview with Ross Douthat on 60 Minutes just days after the Democrats swept the midterms, Donald Trump expressed a willingness to work with the new Congress, including on immigration reform. While the president boasted of his success at deporting “the bad ones,” he also surprised many by adding, “I get hurt because we also took people that had good jobs [. . .] good people working in a landscaping company, or working in a hotel, or doing something, right? And in some cases, paying taxes.” Klion notes Trump also called his policies “too tough” and signaled openness to legal status for some undocumented workers.

The piece contrasts this with Trump’s announcement two days earlier that Stephen Miller would become White House chief of staff. Klion ties Miller to policies from the “Muslim ban” to deportations to El Salvador’s CECOT prison, and doubts he would enable a Democratic immigration deal.

He also expects Miller, faced with a hostile Congress, to lean further on executive orders.

Commentators including The Nation’s Jeet Heer read the move as defiance, with Heer warning of a possible constitutional crisis.

Klion offers a second reading: Trump is running short of trusted aides. Miller, a Hill staffer at 29 when he joined Trump’s first campaign, is prized for loyalty, while insiders reportedly fault outgoing chief of staff Susie Wiles for Republican losses.

With Democrats now holding Congress and governorships in Texas and Georgia, the piece says state police and Guard units may be harder to enlist in federal crackdowns.

Citing a Zeteo report that Miller fears state and local prosecutors after Trump leaves office, Klion suggests he may temper some actions, and likens him to a zombie.

Zombies can still do harm, he adds, so Democrats should prepare for anything from an administrator to a figure claiming a permanent “state of exception.”

Hakeem Jeffries, the likely next House Speaker, ruled out cooperating with Miller, and Chuck Schumer said Trump had “learned nothing” from the defeat.

Klion concludes that without steady public pressure on Democratic leaders, Miller will keep control of key policy levers, returning to his image of a reanimated corpse.

David Klion is a contributing editor at Jewish Currents.
